The workflow these fixes assume

A generated track is a finished stereo render — there is no mix behind it. Anything aimed at one instrument therefore has to go through separation first, and any fix marked with a stem badge below needs this before it will work:

  1. Separate. Effect ▸ OpenVINO AI Effects ▸ Music Separation, using the 4‑stem model. This splits the track into drums, bass, vocals and other — guitars and keys both land in other. Without Intel's OpenVINO plug‑in pack installed, run Demucs or a web separator and bring the results back via File ▸ Import ▸ Audio.
  2. Treat one stem. Click the track you want, apply the effect, leave the others alone. This is the entire point — it is what lets you cut 300 Hz out of the guitars without hollowing out the kick.
  3. Recombine. Select all with Ctrl+A, then Tracks ▸ Mix ▸ Mix and Render.
  4. Export and re‑check. File ▸ Export Audio as WAV, then drop the result back into this page alongside the original and compare them at matched volume.

Separation is not free. It leaves smearing and artefacts, particularly on cymbals and heavily distorted guitars, and a wall of saturated guitar is close to the worst case for it. Use the widest, gentlest moves that solve the problem — a 2 dB shelf across an octave will survive separation, a 9 dB surgical notch will not. If a fix needs more than about 3 dB, regenerating the track is usually the better answer than repairing it.

Fixes without a stem badge work on the whole file and need none of this.

How these numbers are measured, and what they can't tell you

The short version: loudness and peak levels are measured the same way streaming services measure them, so the figures here are the ones that will decide how your track is treated when you upload it. Everything below is the detail behind that.

Loudness follows ITU‑R BS.1770‑4: K‑weighting, 400 ms blocks at 75% overlap, absolute gate at −70 LUFS and a relative gate 10 LU below the ungated mean. Loudness range uses EBU Tech 3342 (3 s windows, −20 LU relative gate, 10th to 95th percentile).

True peak is 4× oversampled with a 65‑tap windowed‑sinc polyphase interpolator. Accuracy is roughly ±0.5 dB worst case near a quarter of the sample rate, which matches what the standard expects of a 4× meter. Anything reading above −0.3 dBTP is worth checking in a dedicated meter before you commit.

Decoding happens through the browser, so lossy files are measured after decode. An MP3 or AAC will often show a true peak above the WAV it came from — that is real, it is what the decoder produces, and it is one reason to leave headroom.

The style targets are reasoned, not sampled. The band-ratio ranges are calibrated against a reference spectrum whose energy falls 3.5 dB per octave and rolls off below 40 Hz and above 16 kHz — a shape most finished records approximate. Genre offsets on top of that come from how the instrumentation behaves, not from a survey of commercial masters. They are useful defaults and they are not ground truth. If you load a record you trust as a reference track, the tool measures against that instead, and you should believe it over the presets every time.

Generated audio behaves differently. It arrives already limited, often already band‑limited, and with a stereo image that can be much narrower than it sounds. Two consequences run through this report: anything destroyed before the render — crushed transients, missing high end — cannot be restored, only worked around or regenerated; and any fix aimed at a single instrument has to go through stem separation, which is itself lossy. Where regenerating is the better answer than repairing, the report says so rather than offering a fix that will not work.

The bandwidth check looks for a knee where the spectrum starts falling faster than 30 dB per octave and keeps falling. A natural rolloff never does that; a codec or a model output ceiling always does. A merely dull mix is not flagged.

Kick punch and snare snap are median band-limited crest factors over 300 ms windows. There is no standard for these, so treat the absolute numbers as relative indicators — comparing two versions of your own track is meaningful, comparing to an abstract target less so.

Mono folding is measured per band as the level change between the summed signal and the average of the two channels. This is what actually happens on a phone speaker or a club PA, and it catches phase problems that a single overall correlation figure hides.

The score is a heuristic. It rewards headroom, sane dynamics, a tonal balance near a pink‑ish reference tilt, and stereo behaviour that survives a mono fold. It has no idea what your track is supposed to sound like. A deliberately crushed loud master and a delicate acoustic master cannot both win. Use the component breakdown, look at the curves, and above all use the loudness‑matched player — measurement narrows the field, ears pick the winner.

Not measured: bit depth and dither (lost during decode), codec artefacts, distortion character, phase rotation, timing, and anything about the arrangement or performance.
